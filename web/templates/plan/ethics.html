A map of a small place carries weight. People will plan their children's days around it, newcomers will form first impressions from it, and organisations will be seen or overlooked because of it. Crescent Compass is built to earn that trust slowly and to keep it.

The principles, and why

Checked before shown. Nothing goes public until a person has checked it against a source, because a wrong opening time or a closed playground costs a family a wasted trip.
Unknown is shown as unknown. If wheelchair access has not been confirmed, the map says so. Guessing "probably accessible" would fail the people who most need to know.
People decide; AI assists. Lumen can suggest and explain, but only a named steward can publish. Responsibility for what the community sees stays with people the community can talk to.
Nothing about children. There are no child accounts and no child profiles, and no child's name, school or home is ever asked for. Families choose age bands as search settings, nothing more.
No tracking. The site never reads a phone's location. Photo GPS data is removed on arrival, and map counts hide any area with fewer than five items, so no single home can be picked out.
No popularity contest. Places are not ranked by clicks or likes. A quiet library programme matters as much as a famous attraction, and small or new organisations are not buried.
Quiet is a choice. Nothing is sent unless a member asks for it, and anyone can browse without joining. Lumen keeps no record of conversations.
Open workings. The rules for recommending, the freshness maths and the cost model are published. Anyone can see why Lumen said what it said.
Rights respected. Map data comes from public sources with recorded licences. Others' work, such as The Quadruplicate's, is credited, and its terms are honoured.

How Lumen works, and its limits

Lumen does

  • Answer in plain words from published listings only
  • Show the source and check date for every place it names
  • Say "I don't know" when the atlas has no answer
  • Recommend only places more than 75 percent likely to be current, and show others with a warning
  • Answer each question on its own, keeping no record of the conversation
  • Draft a correction or attach a photo when a member asks, with a confirm step

Lumen never

  • Publishes, approves or deletes anything
  • Invents a place, an opening time or a price
  • Sees accounts, email addresses, private notes or unpublished items
  • Gives anyone a role or changes a rule
  • Messages anyone on its own
  • Builds a profile of a person, or remembers what they asked

Every Lumen answer is checked sentence by sentence by code on our server, and recorded in the sealed log with the listings it used. If the checks fail, Lumen falls back to a plain, rule-based answer rather than risk an untrue one.

The place: Crescent City and Del Norte County

Del Norte County is home to about 27,000 people, spread between Crescent City and smaller communities: Smith River, Fort Dick, Gasquet, Hiouchi and Klamath. It lies within the ancestral lands of the Tolowa Dee-ni' and Yurok peoples, and includes the Tolowa Dee-ni' Nation at Smith River, the Elk Valley and Resighini Rancherias, and part of the Yurok Reservation. The county holds some of the cleanest rivers and oldest redwoods in the country, a working harbour, and the memory of the 1964 tsunami. Distances are long, services are spread thin, and many families rely on word of mouth to find what exists.

Crescent Compass sits alongside existing community efforts rather than replacing them. The Quadruplicate, built locally by Daniel Friedman, already gathers civic information for Crescent City; the library, schools, Family Resource Center and tribal programmes already know their communities far better than any map can.

Who the map is for

  • Caregivers of children from birth to 18: parents, grandparents, foster families and anyone looking after a child.
  • Newcomers finding their feet in a new place.
  • Local organisations that want families to find them.

Who is represented, and who may be missing

Today, every listing comes from an official website. That means organisations with websites are over-represented, and much of what makes Del Norte work is not yet on the map. We expect these to be missing or thin:

  • Informal and word-of-mouth resources: church groups, parent networks, swap meets and volunteer-run programmes.
  • Services and gatherings for families who speak Spanish or other languages.
  • Tribal programmes and places, which are for each Nation to share or not.
  • The outlying communities, especially Klamath, Gasquet and Smith River.
  • Families without reliable internet, who may never see the map at all.
  • Accurate accessibility detail, which is mostly "unknown" today.
  • Families with particular needs, such as those visiting relatives at Pelican Bay State Prison, or teenagers looking for their own spaces.

What we do about it

  • Publish a "what is not here yet" view, showing which communities and kinds of place have few verified listings.
  • Track which communities our contributors and stewards come from, compared with the county's population, and reach out where a community is under-represented.
  • Invite long-standing community members to add places they know, through the pilot with Daniel, Sasha, Kim and others.
  • Ask each Nation before showing anything about its lands, programmes or people, following the CARE principles for Indigenous data: collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility and ethics.
  • Offer printable lists through libraries and the Family Resource Center for families who are not online.
  • Never describe the map as complete.

How the map changes, and who changes it

  • Members suggest places, photos and corrections. Nothing they send is public until approved.
  • Stewards, starting with Kim, Sasha, Daniel and Bill, add or amend the map at any time through Lumen, and clear general updates at a monthly round. Every decision is recorded with its reason.
  • Time changes the map too: confidence in each listing fades until it is re-checked, so stale information stops being recommended without anyone having to notice.
  • The rules themselves change only by a decision of the stewards and the wider group Kim chooses, recorded in the log and explained publicly. See the Future tab for how that could work.

A growing trust network, never the single source of truth

The map starts with four founding stewards. Over time, members with a proven record are invited to join them, especially from communities the map does not yet reach well. A wider, more varied network keeps the map honest. It does not make the map complete.

More local knowledge. Each new steward brings places and corrections that no website lists, and spots mistakes sooner.
Trust is earned and can fade. Track records weigh recent work most. A steward who steps away hands over, and old trust does not linger.
Checks and balances. Every change names the steward who made it and its source. Sensitive changes need a second steward, and disagreements are recorded rather than hidden.
Honesty stays visible. Every listing links to its original source and shows when it was last confirmed. Lumen says "according to the library's website, checked 3 May" rather than speaking as the authority.
Pointing beyond itself. The map sends people to the library, the school, the Nation or the organisation itself. When sources disagree, it shows both or says it is unsure.
Saying what it does not cover. It invites corrections from everyone and says plainly where it is thin. The map stays a careful sketch of a living place, and the territory always has the last word.
