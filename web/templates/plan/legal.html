Accounts in Alex's name, with Kim in control

Holding the accounts in Alex's name is the easiest way to build, and it is lawful, as long as the contract says Alex holds them for Kim. In both countries, the person who decides what the data is for, Kim, is the one responsible for it. Who pays the bill or holds the login does not change that.

What US law asks of Kim, as a private individual

The US has no general data protection law for private individuals, and no register of "data controllers". The state privacy laws, such as California's, only apply to businesses far above this size, usually 100,000 people's data or millions in revenue, and most leave out non-profits. Kim is well below every threshold. What does apply is short:

  • Tell the truth about data. The privacy page must match what the site really does.
  • Keep it reasonably secure, and if sign-in details are ever exposed, tell the members affected. California's law splits this neatly: whoever looks after data for its owner tells the owner straight away, and the owner tells the people affected within 30 days. So Alex tells Kim, and Kim tells members.
  • Collect nothing about children, which the site already does by design.

The privacy notice law names the site's owner as responsible, and leaves out anyone who hosts or manages a site on the owner's behalf. Kim is the owner and Alex the manager, so the law sits with Kim, as intended.

Alex's full access while building

Alex can have full access to everything throughout. That is normal for the person building and running a site. During the eight-week build there are no real members yet, only public listings and test accounts, so there is almost no personal data to protect. Once members join, Alex keeps full access on three conditions: he uses it only for the site, his accounts use two-step sign-in, and he keeps no copies of members' data on his own computer.

The no-children rule, events and other map features

Children. No child accounts and no child details: the main US children's privacy law does not apply to a site for adults that collects nothing about children. Sign-up now asks people to confirm they are 18 or over. If a child's account is ever found, it is deleted.
Public events. Taken from organisations' own websites and calendars, with credit, and checked by a steward. Each one links back to its source.
Group meetups. The site is a noticeboard, not an organiser. The terms say it does not run, supervise or vet meetups, outdoor activities carry their own risks, and parents stay responsible for their children. Meeting places are always public places. If Kim's organisation ever ran supervised children's activities itself, California's background-check law for youth organisations would apply. Listing events and helping families meet does not trigger it.
RSVPs and reminders. Only a count of people coming is shown. Reminders go by email, only to people who asked. No text messages, because US texting law needs special consent records.
Photos. Places, not people. No identifiable children, no faces without permission, and no homes. Location data is removed on arrival, and every photo is checked by a steward. Member photos are never used for promotion or fundraising.
Lumen. Answers only from published listings, says it is automated and can be wrong, and has no personality designed to form a relationship. It keeps no record of conversations: each question is answered on its own, and the log never stores what people type. The map stays accurate through the back-end instead: stewards' updates through Lumen, the monthly website checks, and the freshness clock on every listing.
Your data. Members can now download everything held about them, or delete their account themselves, from My atlas. The sealed log never records names or emails, only a member number.
The map itself. Map data comes from public sources with recorded licences and credit. No children's homes or locations, and no sensitive nature sites.

The contract between Alex and Kim

A single written agreement, signed electronically by both, is enough in both countries. It needs ten parts:

  1. Who. Kim, as an individual or on behalf of her organisation, and Alex trading as Honami, an independent contractor working from the UK with his own tools.
  2. What. The eight-week scope and milestones from the brief, with written acceptance of each milestone. A milestone counts as accepted if Kim does not reply within an agreed time, such as 10 working days.
  3. Money. $10,000 fixed, in US dollars, in milestone payments. Running costs ($500 to $1,000 a year) are paid by Kim directly, and any paid service needs her written approval first.
  4. Ownership. Once paid, Alex assigns the code and content to Kim in writing. His pre-existing tools are licensed to her, and open-source parts keep their own licences. Avoid the phrase "work made for hire", which has a special meaning in California employment law.
  5. Accounts. Alex holds the technical accounts on Kim's behalf. Kim, Daniel and Bill have full access at all times, and Alex moves any account into their names whenever they ask, or when the work ends.
  6. Data. A short data processing section: Alex acts only on Kim's instructions, keeps data secure and confidential, lists the services used (server host, email, Anthropic), tells Kim of any breach within 48 hours, helps with members' requests, and deletes his access at the end.
  7. Confidentiality both ways.
  8. Care and limits. Alex works with reasonable skill and care and fixes defects for an agreed period. He gives no promise about third-party software or the accuracy of listings, and his liability is capped at the fee.
  9. Ending and disputes. Either side can end it on notice, with payment for work done. If they disagree, they talk first, then try online mediation. Choose one governing law: England and Wales suits Alex, California suits Kim. English law, with any claim brought where the other person lives, is a fair middle way.
  10. Ongoing work. Later support from the UK goes in a separate short support agreement or change orders, with an agreed rate and Kim's approval before each piece of paid work.

Kim signs as a private individual, so UK consumer law may treat her as a consumer. That means the contract must be fair and plainly worded, and she may have a 14-day right to cancel before work starts. Signing on behalf of her organisation, if she has one, makes this simpler.

Tax and invoicing, for Alex

  • UK tax: the fee is ordinary Honami income on the Self Assessment return, converted to pounds at the rate received.
  • US tax: none, because the work is done wholly in the UK. Kim needs no tax form as a private payer. Alex can offer a W-8BEN form if anyone asks.
  • VAT: design, data processing and hosting work for a customer outside the UK is generally outside UK VAT, and the registration threshold is £90,000. Worth a quick check with an accountant.
  • Invoice wording: "Services performed wholly in the UK. Outside the scope of UK VAT." Invoice in US dollars, and a service such as Wise avoids high bank fees.
  • Insurance: professional indemnity insurance is not required by law. If Alex has a policy, check that it covers US clients.

The minimum to do before members can join

  1. Sign the contract, including the data processing section. Cost: an optional review by a lawyer.
  2. Alex runs the UK regulator's five-minute fee self-check. £0, or £52 a year.
  3. Kim approves the Privacy page and adds a contact email.
  4. Publish short terms of use: 18 and over, photo rules, not an organiser of meetups, and a removal request route.
  5. Hold the accounts in Alex's name for Kim, with Kim, Daniel and Bill as full administrators, and accept each service's standard data processing terms.
  6. Keep sign-in on the site's own server with secure connections and two-step sign-in for stewards and Alex.
  7. Write a one-page breach plan: stop it, record it, tell Kim, then tell affected members and ask them to reset passwords.
  8. Keep a one-page record of what data is held, where it is, and who can reach it.

Optional: a copyright agent

Registering a copyright agent with the US Copyright Office costs $6 every three years. It is not required. It gives extra legal protection if someone uploads a photo they do not own, and it means publishing a contact name and postal address. Because stewards check every photo first, and anything is removed promptly on request, it can wait until photo uploads are busy, if it is needed at all.
