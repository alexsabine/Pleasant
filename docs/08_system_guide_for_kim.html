How the atlas works, who looks after it, and what it costs

A plain guide to the parts behind the website, the people who keep it honest, and a year of running costs for twenty members, set against the $1,500 ceiling.

The short answer

  • The atlas works today without any AI. Compass answers from the verified listings with fixed rules, so its running cost is the website alone, about $340 a year on a small server.
  • Adding AI is cheap if it only rewords checked facts. With a small model such as Claude Haiku, Gemini Flash-Lite or GPT-5 nano, twenty members cost $1 to $35 a year in AI, even with heavy use.
  • The most capable models fit too, with a cap. Claude Opus wording every answer costs about $520 a year at typical use, and a daily limit of about 100 worded answers guarantees the year stays under $1,500.
  • Our own AI computer does not fit the first year. It needs $1,800 to $2,300 of hardware up front. Spread over three years it costs about $860 a year, plus the website.
  • Stewards are people, never the AI. Two named stewards approve everything that goes public. The AI cannot publish, verify, or give anyone a role.

The big picture

Think of the atlas as a small public library. The website is the reading room people walk into. The server is the librarian who fetches things. The records are the shelves, kept in a database. Stewards work at a separate desk where new material is checked before it goes on the public shelf.

Nothing reaches the public shelf without a person checking it. Every change and every Compass answer is written into a log that cannot be quietly edited later.

How Compass answers a question

Compass follows the same six steps every time. Steps 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 are fixed rules that run on our own server and cost nothing. Step 4 is the only place an AI model can be used, and only to make the wording friendlier.

What an "API call" is

An API call is one request from our server to an AI company's computer: we send some text, it sends text back, and we pay by the amount of text. Text is measured in tokens, where a token is roughly three quarters of a word. Prices are quoted per million tokens, with words sent in costing less than words sent back.

For Compass, one question uses at most two calls. The first turns a question the rules could not read into filters such as age and cost. The second rewords the checked answer. A typical question with both calls sends about 3,300 tokens and receives about 900, including the model's own working.

The AI never sees accounts, email addresses, saved places, private notes, unpublished drafts, or anything about a child. It never writes to the records, never publishes, and never answers on its own when our checks disagree.

What a steward does each week

The review queue lists which listings most need a re-check, ordered by how likely they are to have changed and how much a fix is worth. For about 60 listings this comes to about three checks a week, roughly 35 minutes. Stewards also clear the suggestion queue and, once a month, read the log and decide any rule changes together.

Open questions for Kim: who the two stewards are; whether system-wide changes (new categories, switching on AI or WhatsApp) need both stewards or a wider group; and whether contributors come by request, invitation, or both.

How joining works

Joining is optional. Everything public works without an account.

  1. Browse firstThe map, listings and Compass are open to anyone, with no sign-up and no location request.
  2. JoinA username, email and password, plus a box confirming the person is an adult caregiver, resident or community member. There are no child accounts.
  3. What help would be welcome?Family support, local orientation, community events, practical guidance. Skippable.
  4. What to look forAge bands such as "Preschool (3 to 5)", budget, step-free access only, closest community. These are search settings, not a child profile. Skippable.
  5. How much Compass should sayA few suggestions, a standard shortlist, or everything; whether Compass may use these settings; email summaries stay off unless turned on. Skippable.
  6. My atlasSaved places, settings to change or clear, and a button to ask a steward for contributor rights.

Never asked: a child's name, birth date, school, or home address. No location is read from the phone. A member can tell Compass "forget that" at any time.

A year of running costs

The estimate covers twenty onboarded members for 52 weeks. Each member asks Compass a number of questions a week and sends a few suggestions or corrections. Three levels of use are compared: light (5 questions, a suggestion a month), typical (15 questions, 1 suggestion) and heavy (40 questions, 3 suggestions).

The total has three parts. Hosting is the website and database. AI calls are paid per use. Own hardware applies only if we buy or rent our own AI computer. Four ways of using AI are compared:

Running our own AI computer

An open model can run on a computer we own, so no question ever leaves our hands. Models in the 20 to 30 billion parameter range, such as Qwen, gpt-oss-20b or Mistral Small, word checked answers well and need a machine with plenty of memory. Twenty members ask at most a few questions an hour, so a single quiet machine is enough.

OptionHardwareElectricity a yearPer year over 3 yearsFirst-year cash

Electricity is priced at 26 cents per kWh, the Del Norte average, for a machine left on day and night. The website still needs its own server, because a computer at home depends on home internet and power, and opening it to the public adds security work. Someone also has to update and look after the machine, and that time is not in these figures.

Renting the same open model from a hosting company such as Groq or Together gives most of the benefit with no hardware: about $17 a year at typical use with every answer worded.

Recommendation for the first year

Run the site on a small server and use AI only when the rules are stuck, with a small model. That costs about $340 to $400 a year and leaves about $1,100 for surprises, a second domain, or trying a stronger model for a few months. If a stronger model is wanted for every answer, Claude Sonnet or Opus fits at typical use, protected by a daily limit so heavy months cannot overshoot.

Keep our own AI computer as a later step. It fits the yearly ceiling only once the hardware is paid for, for example from a one-off grant. After that its running cost is electricity, $90 to $275 a year, plus the website.

Avoid sending family questions to providers outside the US or EU, even when cheaper. DeepSeek is listed for comparison only.

How the maths keeps it smooth, cheap and honest

The atlas already contains a small set of published formulas. Each one does a practical job.

The AI never decides what is true. Whether to recommend a listing is a calculation: the chance it is still current, worked out from when it was last checked and how often places like it change. Compass recommends only above 75 percent. Below that it shows the listing with a warning, or says it does not know.
A cheap model is safe because every sentence is checked. Each AI sentence must name the listings it relies on. Any sentence that names an unknown place, claims access that is not verified, or adds a fact with no listing is removed. The safety comes from the check, so we do not need to pay for the most capable model.
Ask the AI only when it changes the answer. The rules already understand most questions. In a test of 34 typical questions they understood 33. Calling the AI only when the rules are stuck cuts AI calls, and AI cost, by about 90 percent compared with wording every answer.
A daily limit turns the budget into a guarantee. If a worded answer costs about 3 cents with Claude Opus, a limit of about 100 worded answers a day keeps the year under $1,500 with the website included. Past the limit, Compass answers from the plain template. The limit is a setting stewards can change.
Short memory, long records. Compass remembers only the last six turns of a conversation, and only which listings it showed. The long-term memory is the database and the sealed log, which cost nothing per question. Each question therefore costs the same in year three as on day one.
The re-check schedule is worked out, not guessed. The freshness formulas tell stewards which listings to re-check first. About three checks a week keep every listing recommendable, which keeps the steward workload small and steady.
Every answer can be audited. Each Compass answer and each publish is written into a chained log, where each entry seals the one before. Changing an old entry breaks the chain, and a single command shows whether it is intact.
Privacy by design. No child data exists to leak. Counts on the map are grouped into hexagon cells about 0.7 square kilometres across, and any cell with fewer than five items is hidden, so no single home or person can be picked out.

Assumptions and sources

All figures are estimates made on 16 October 2026 from stated assumptions; nothing has been measured with real members yet. Prices change often, and price-comparison sites disagree by up to about 20 percent. The model behind every number is in the project repository as backend/examples/cost_model.py, with its output in research/09_cost_model.md, so it can be re-run with new prices.

  • OpenAI: BenchLM, Silicon Data
